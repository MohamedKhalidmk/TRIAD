\begin{abstract}
\raggedright
Patients can wait and deteriorate while information about them is missed, delayed, or scattered across records, and physicians spend much of their time piecing it together. We propose TRIAD, a data model that connects the information recorded by nurses and physicians so it can be shared and updated as it changes, and an agent can retrieve it directly rather than search for it. Code: \url{github.com/MohamedKhalidmk/TRIAD} (\mbox{CC BY-NC-SA 4.0}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% Khaled's section: his own text, AI-edited for clarity only (Level C).
% Originals in Appendix A.
When a patient calls a clinic, the information they give may be incomplete or inaccurate before a nurse summarizes it. Patients may be prioritized incorrectly, so urgent cases wait; a review of emergency triage reports nurse accuracy between 59.3\% and 82\%~\cite{triage}. Physicians also lose time to wrong or outdated information in slow systems, spending 49\% of their office day on electronic records and desk work~\cite{sinsky2016}.

Patient information comes from the patient's report, the provincial pharmacy record (PharmaNet)~\cite{pharmanet}, the nurse's assessment and recommendation (SBAR)~\cite{sbar}, and the physician's decision to accept, reject, or build on it. Because this history is created by different people and organizations, records can disagree. The physician then cannot easily tell whether a record still reflects what the patient is actually taking~\cite{ismp}, who recorded and verified it, and whether the nurse's recommendation was accepted.

FHIR gives systems a common structure for exchanging records, but not a shared, machine-interpretable account of how records relate; it points at conflicts rather than resolving them. Medication information is scattered across orders, dispenses, and patient reports, and clinicians reconcile it manually~\cite{fhirmeds}.


\section{Motivation}
Hospitals are slow to use agents in clinical work, for two reasons. First, an agent cannot keep all of this data in its conversation window. In a long context, the agent can miss that a fact changed and use the old fact.

Second, patient data is sensitive. Personally Identifiable Information (PII), such as a name or a health card number, becomes Protected Health Information (PHI) when it is linked to health data. Laws such as HIPAA in the United States, and PIPEDA and PHIPA in Canada, control the use of PHI. A hospital cannot use an agent that shows PHI to the wrong person or does not obey these laws.

An Electronic Health Record (EHR) is designed for people to read. It does not show the agent who asserted what and what replaced what, so the agent must infer this at runtime. When the inference is wrong, nobody can find the cause, staff stop trusting the agent, and hospitals do not use it.

\subsection{Motivating Example}
A patient comes to the emergency room with shortness of breath. The nurse assesses the patient and records a possible asthma attack. One hour later, the physician examines the patient and revises the assessment to possible heart failure. The physician also tells the agent that the father of the patient died of a heart attack. This fact is not in the EHR yet.

In the evening, the patient asks the agent about the plan for their care. The conversation is now long. The asthma assessment appears many times, and the revision appears once. The agent answers from the old assessment and tells the patient that an inhaler is the plan.

With TRIAD, the agent starts at the patient and finds the current assessment one or two hops away in an RDF~\cite{rdf} knowledge graph. It sees that the physician replaced the assessment of the nurse, and it finds the family history. If its answer is wrong, we can trace the error to a specific relationship. The agent shows the patient only approved data, and staff who do not care for this patient get no access to their PHI.

\subsection{Motivation: Original Transcript}
\textit{Spoken transcript by Asish A. George.}

I want to talk about the motivation for the project. There are different levels of motivation. First, in multi-agent systems, let's say a doctor has an agent to talk about patient data, and a nurse has an agent to talk about the patient data. These two agents may have fragmented memory about the patient data itself. We don't want different people to have fragmented memory about the same element. We want to have a cohesive memory within an agentic system so that patient care happens in a much easier way.

That aside, agentic systems are not being accepted or adopted into real healthcare systems as much, for different reasons. One is that the data is fragmented across different systems. The second is the concern of privacy, whether a data leak will happen. And third, there are strict regulations like HIPAA, and PHIPA in Canada, where we have to adhere to certain laws to support the healthcare environment.

We want to create a system or a data model that solves most of these problems. In an agentic system, we want a really fast lookup. So having a triple store, or RDF-based store, where we can query a patient's data would be beneficial for us.

The second is in terms of the data store. Like I said, there are multiple fragmented data stores, but the most common is FHIR data. It might not be widely adopted, but it is the global standard for interoperability for healthcare data. So we could model the agent in such a way that any FHIR data source can be attached to the agent, so that we can load FHIR data into the RDF store at any time, and the agent can operate within that data store.

The third is in terms of data privacy. Even though we are using a graph database to hold the relations, we also try to ensure that no data leaks, that one patient's data is not shown to another patient, and that only the practitioners who are responsible for the patient can see the patient.

This is the motivation behind the project and what we want to do with it.
